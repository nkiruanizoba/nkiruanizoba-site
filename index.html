Generative AI for visual effects

Turning natural-language descriptions into production-ready VFX.

I've led a portfolio of AI-assisted world-building initiatives focused on some of the repetitive work involved in creating and editing large game environments.

Developer productivity

Using AI and production data to make game development more reliable.

One part of my work has focused on the systems behind game development itself.

I led work on an LLM-based diagnostic tool that helps developers understand build failures and get to likely fixes faster. In parallel, I developed a branch-stability measurement system that made recurring sources of instability visible and helped teams intervene earlier.

That work contributed to a 15% reduction in build failure rate over a year.

What interested me most was not simply adding AI to developer tooling. It was understanding where developers were losing time, what information they needed at the moment something went wrong, and how better feedback loops could change the underlying system.

My role: I led the diagnostics tool and delivered the branch stability and analytics platform, including its KPIs and reporting.

  • Developer tools
  • LLMs
  • Reliability
  • Product analytics

Knowing when not to ship AI

A multimodal QA product that made sense technically, but not economically.

I explored a multimodal AI system designed to detect localization and UI issues from in-game screenshots.

Technically, the approach worked. But when I modeled the cost of running it at the scale the workflow required, the economics didn't justify continuing.

So we didn't.

It reinforced something I think is increasingly important in AI product development: proving that a model can perform a task is only one part of the product decision. Quality, latency, operating cost, workflow fit and the value of the problem all matter.

My role: I ran the evaluation and cost modeling behind the decision to stop.

  • Multimodal AI
  • Product economics
  • Build vs. buy
  • Product judgment

Sift

An insights engine for messy text.

Sift answers questions in plain English and surfaces the relevant insights, with every finding linked back to the reviews that support it.

I built it on more than 26,000 public Steam reviews spanning major game updates. It clusters feedback into themes, compares how sentiment and issues change across updates, and links generated insights back to the underlying reviews.

I also built hand-labeled evaluations to test whether its citations and conclusions were actually grounded in the source material.

Sift started with a question I kept coming back to: how do you use an LLM to synthesize thousands of opinions without asking the user to simply trust what it says?

Rung

An AI math tutor designed to guide students to think, not just output answers.

Rung explores a problem I find particularly interesting in AI and learning: a model that can answer almost anything can easily do too much of the work for the learner.

I designed Rung around hints rather than answers. The system identifies likely misconceptions, tracks what a learner appears to understand and adapts the next intervention accordingly.

Building it has meant thinking about more than the conversational layer: deterministic grading, misconception detection, knowledge tracing, evaluation, latency, guardrails and the boundary between being helpful and taking over the reasoning process.

About

Strategy background. Builder habits.

I've spent most of my career working on messy problems where the answer isn't obvious.

I started in analytics, where I learned to turn data into decisions, and later moved into strategy consulting, where I worked on questions that rarely arrived neatly defined. Over time, I became less interested in stopping at the recommendation and more interested in what came next: deciding what to build, making the tradeoffs and seeing whether it actually worked.

That pulled me toward product.

I went on to earn my Master of Business Administration from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, then moved into games, where I've worked across player-facing products, developer systems and AI.

AI has made that work especially interesting to me because the technology changes quickly while the fundamentals of good product judgment do not. A model can be impressive and still be the wrong product. A prototype can work and still fail in a real workflow. And sometimes the most important decision is deciding not to build something at all.

Outside of my day job, I build products myself. It keeps me close to the details: prompts, evals, failure cases, data, latency, cost and all the small decisions that disappear when a product is reduced to a roadmap slide.

Increasingly, I'm also interested in learning: how intelligent systems can help people reason, practice and develop understanding without simply doing the thinking for them.

Across all of it, the question I keep coming back to is pretty simple:

What would have to be true for this to actually work?

That's usually where I like to start.
